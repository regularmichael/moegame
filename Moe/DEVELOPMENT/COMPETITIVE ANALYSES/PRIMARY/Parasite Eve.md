Platform: PS1
Genre: Turn-based RPG, strategy, survival horror, science fiction, body horror, modern setting
Difficult: Easy to medium

Brief Synopsis:
Parasite Eve is a turn-based horror RPG released in 1998. The story follows police officer Aya Brea, who finds herself one of few survivors of a massacre involving spontaneous human combustion. She learns that she is immune to the powers of the villain EVE, who is a sentient mitochondrial organism bent on liberating all mitochondria. As players progress the game, they watch Eve slowly transform into something grotesque, and Aya is only one who can stop her.

There are a lot of cool things that this game did with a unique combination of RPG and survival horror, as well as the time that it came out. There are many things that can help lay the groundwork for a core Moe game experience.

**CHARACTERS**
Players control Aya Brea and only Aya Brea for the game. Through various flashbacks, her past and her connection to various events and characters (including Eve) are revealed to the player. Players will frequently come across Eve and fight her, and despite her repeated appearances, her consistent power growth and further deterioration make her a reasonably effective villain. 

The supporting cast includes Aya's fellow police officers (and some of their families), as well as some scientists and civilians. The characters are rarely three dimensional, but they are portrayed realistically, and that helps make them endearing or memorable despite their depth. I will say that there are very obvious "gag" characters, and consistently having to interact with them can be tedious.

**NARRATIVE**
The narrative is simple, but effective. Setting the scene at each major plot point usually relies on a lot of scientific jargon to make things complex or mysterious, which I think is fine for the era of game this came from. The overall goal as Aya is just to catch Eve, so you follow her trail of destruction and learn more than you'll ever want to about mitochondria along the way. It's a good set up that sustains a great level of tension as you seek out Eve, wondering what she's up to, and the release you feel when she leaves the scene.

The background of the world is told through interactions with characters, flashback cutscenes, and files or notes found in the world. Notably, the files and notes are optional, but help provide a lot of context. As far as I know (~1h30m in), getting information this way only provides flavor. There's no mechanical benefit. For example, an early stage lets you find the diary of the person whom Eve emerged from. The diary discusses medications the host needed to take. This is tragic, but another character mentions this during a debrief session minutes later. So, as far as I know, it doesn't matter if you pick up the note or not because you'll learn this information either way. I'm not holding that against this 30-year-old game, but I do want to make sure that notes are effective in a core Moe game.

**CHARACTER PROGRESSION**
Aya progresses by accumulating experience points at the end of combat. When she reaches a basic threshold, she levels up, which often rewards players with:
* Increased stats 
* Additional spells

I don't intend to make this a leveling up game, but the important thing to know is that as Aya becomes more experienced with the world (through combat, investigation, etc.) she necessarily becomes more competent and is able to use more powerful supernatural abilities in combat. This is a mechanical expression of Aya's capabilities as a character and her role in the narrative. 

Players can upgrade their weapons and defensive equipment using single-use items or mods they find in the world. Interestingly, players also earn "BP," or bonus points, which they can use to increase inventory size, among other information. The amount of BP needed in order to be used isn't really communicated in the game, and how get it is unclear as well. I wish the game had a reminder that you have BP to spend, at the very least.

Players are given a range of weapons and armor they can use. These are part of a surprisingly complex modification system that lets players adjust the number of bullets that can be fired per round, increase critical hit damage, allow players to use medicine automatically if they would have died, and so one. This is really cool because players are given the opportunity to choose a favorite loadout and explore it, rather than just replacing gear with the next best item. Using this system effectively really makes it so players can express their skills and think about what they bring into combat.

Unfortunately, the same care isn't really given to the magical attacks, which are just unlocked, with stronger ones available as the player progresses. That makes them feel more disappointing than anything else, especially since an item in the inventory is usually stronger than an analogous spell, albeit at the cost that it can only be used once.

**ENEMY DESIGN**
The enemies are surreal, grotesque, malformed versions of typical creatures. Rats grow large, their jaws for their way out of their mouth. Snakes grow much larger. As the game progresses, the enemies become more and more twisted, barely resembling real creatures. Eve herself grows more distended as the game progresses.

These are a good basis for enemy designs in the first game. Magic twists and distorts people who are subject to it, especially the magic used by the powerful but untrained Annamarie. The way these enemies grow and change, as well as their direct modes of attack, are a strong philosophy to adopt.

**EXPLORATION**
Exploration is usually limited to where the narrative has guided the player. Most areas have a multiple paths you can explore by finding a key or investigating your surrounding, such as pressing the action button near a crack in the wall. The investigation of the environment here is satisfying, as it allows you to learn more about the world and really supports the idea that you are playing an investigator. For example, in the the early areas, you find yourself in a theatre's prop room. Examining the props closet will cause a bird to pop out, who just sits there and says things. It's a cool touch.

The areas are mostly empty outside of combat, except for when you find bodies, characters to interact with, or chests with items. As you navigate, items you find will take spots in your inventory, which is a single screen of the character menu. Key items, weapons, medicine, junk - anything you pick up will live in this inventory. There is no way to stack or combine like items - if you have 4 medicine 1 items, then they take up 4 inventory slots. 

This is a classic survival horror trope that I don't necessarily hate, but there's some tedium here as you'll find yourself having to drop or discard items. Empty chests allow you to stash items in there, which is a neat idea, but if you really wanted it you'd have to backtrack, which can expose you to random encounters. (The exception here is ammo, which is stashed in an ammo box - a very smart concession, I think.)

Levels are full of rich detail and I think they beckon players to explore. The atmosphere is so strong in this game and I found myself really enjoying (most of the) areas I came across. However, I find that Aya moves extremely slowly, so if you're lost or find yourself needing to backtrack, there is some frustration as you tend to stay lost or backtrack much longer than you may need to just because of her movement speed.

While exploring the world, you'll find phones with blinking lights. These are save points, and they act as phone you can use to call your partners and update them on your situation. These conversations don't happen in the game, but I think the narrative touch is really nice. Also, I like restricted saved as a horror game concept, so I like using this. 

**COMBAT**
Combat is turn-based, initiated randomly as players explore the level or as part of narrative set pieces. Aya is the only controllable character, but she often has more than 1 enemy to deal with at a time.

In combat, players have 3 components to their UI: 
* a bar that, once filled, players can stop to perform an action like attack, use an item, or cast a spell
* HP, with current maximum
* PE, or "parasite energy". This is essentially a mana bar that fills up over time.
	* It ONLY replenishes in combat, for some reason.

Once the time bar fills up, players can take an action. The world freezes while players make a decision, allowing some reprieve from combat. (It stays frozen while she uses an item or a spell as well.) Performing an action in combat requires a particular resource. For all ranged weapons, that includes bullets; for items, it requires having the item in your inventory (as well as having the space in your inventory to hold items in the first place); and for magic, it requires some PE. Balancing these is a great idea in principal that really emphasizes its survival horror background. 

While making an attack, players will see a dome pop up that represents effective attack range. This means that players have to be in range in order for attacks to land. There's also a UI that pops up that lets you know your total number of bullets, as well as how many shots your gun has. 

Players can split your bullets between all of the enemies on the field, or pump them all into one enemy. This is an interesting choice players have to make because if the player has, say, 3 shots in a gun, and the first bullet kills the enemy, then the other two shots just don't happen. Bullets aren't wasted, but you WILL need to wait for your action bar to fill up before you can choose another enemy to fire at. 

Additionally, while you're firing, Aya stays still on the field, which makes her vulnerable to attacks. She'll still complete her firing queue, she'll just take damage in between shots. If enemies have two attacks right after each other, then that stagger is extended. However, Aya can also do this to enemies,, so if she has multiple shots she can use, an observant player can use shots strategically to delay enemy attacks so she can move out of the way after she fires. (In theory, anyway.)

When players are NOT performing an action, they can move Aya around the battlefield in real time. This allows players to maintain their distance and dodge attacks, as well as get better vantage points when they can attack. This is really cool, and it's not a system that is used very frequently in either RPGs or Horror games. Enemies can also freely move around the battle field, which means they can move out of the range of Aya's attacks or press an advantage in tight corridors. 

At the end of combat, players receive XP for each enemy they kill, as well as items like ammo or healing items, as well as junk that just takes up inventory space. This seems to only be there to punish inobservant players who just click "take all", which I don't mind,

The system here is very interesting, and I think it could be useful if we decide to go in an isometric or turn-based route. Even outside of those perspectives, there's some cool stuff here that we could include, such as the replenishing mana, effective attack ranges, and resource requirements for attacks. 

I do also want to note that the majority of the horror and tension comes from the way bodies are contorted in this game, especially enemies. Many times I found myself coming across a new enemy and saying, "What the fuck is this?" Their unusual bodies made them feel very unpredictable on first encounter, and thankfully areas are short enough that there is no short supply of new monsters to be caught off guard by.

However, I feel like this game is extremely generous with resources, especially PE replenishment and ammo, so I rarely felt pressured during combat. When I *was* pressed, usually during a boss fight or surrounded by enemies with status effects, the fact that Aya is the only character was sort of problematic insofar as players may find themselves in a loop of constantly trying to top off their health or remove status effects, rather than whittling down enemy HP.  

**UI**
Since this an RPG, UI elements should be discussed. When I think about it, there are 3 major UI elements that players become familiar with: the status menu, where you can see Aya's inventory or statistics; the combat UI, describe above; and the attack-specific UI, which includes information about how many bullets you have, who you're targeting, and how you assign attacks. 

It's interesting to think about it this way because they all feel disparate in appearance and utility, but they all mostly work well together. However, the status menu is a bit convoluted and has a LOT of options that make it feel overwhelming once all of your systems are available to you. 

**TAKEAWAY**
Parasite Eve is a one-of-a-kind game that could provide a wealth of inspiration for this game.

The things that a Moe game could take of Parasite Eve include:
* Encouraging exploration through narrative and cool level design
* A replenishing magical attack system
* Survival horror elements like a limited inventory and strange enemies
* Persistent confrontations with an enemy that keeps players on their toes
* Rewarding players for paying attention to character position and enemy behaviors

I think that overall, Parasite Eve is also a really good *warning* for a Moe game as well. Aya Brea is a very competent character with a lot of skills, and sometimes the systems in this game make it so that she has a lot of answers virtually anything. I know that is a necessity for an RPG with just one character in the party, but the caution here is that the tension of the game is rarely driven by mechanics. I didn't really feel scared since I had a kitted out assault rifle with over 200 bullets.
